Life, not brochure copy

What it is actually like to live here.

You are not enrolling in a course. You are moving to a country where you do not speak the language, do not know the systems, and will be doing your own administration for the first time. Here is what that really involves.

Choose your city deliberately

Three very different
student experiences.

Where you study changes your monthly budget by several hundred euros and changes your daily life far more than that. Do not default to Athens simply because it is the name you know.

Population ~3.6m

Athens

The largest choice of programmes, the most international cohorts, the best flight connections, and the highest rents. Loud, layered, genuinely cosmopolitan. Best for students who want opportunity density and do not mind a big city's friction.

Population ~1m

Thessaloniki

Greece's student city by reputation — a large young population, a compact waterfront centre, and noticeably lower costs than Athens. Colder winters, fewer direct long-haul flights, a stronger sense of community.

Crete

Smallest scale, mildest winters, strongest seasonal tourism employment, and the lowest living costs of the three. The trade-off is a narrower programme choice and a quieter year outside the summer.

The unglamorous part

Six things that will occupy
your first month.

Nobody warns students about this list, and it is the list that determines whether the first semester feels manageable or overwhelming.

Finding somewhere to live

Most students arrive into short-term accommodation and find a permanent place in the first two or three weeks. Expect to pay a deposit of one to two months. Never transfer money for a flat you or someone you trust has not seen — advance-fee rental scams specifically target arriving international students, every single September.

AFM, AMKA and a bank account

The tax number and social security number are the keys to everything administrative: legal employment, healthcare, a proper rental contract, a bank account. Get them early. Every week you delay pushes back everything that depends on them.

The residence permit appointment

Lodged at the local Aliens and Immigration Department in your first weeks. Bring everything, bring copies, and expect the process to take longer than you were told. This is normal and not a sign that something is wrong.

Working, if you choose to

Twenty hours a week during term, in registered employment, once your permit, AFM and AMKA are in place. Hospitality and tourism absorb the most English-speaking student labour, especially from May to September. Treat it as living-cost support, never as a tuition plan.

Healthcare

Your insurance must remain valid for the whole permit period — this is checked at renewal. Know before you need it: where your nearest hospital is, what your policy actually covers, and how you claim.

The part people do not say out loud

Weeks four to eight are usually the hardest. The novelty has worn off, the first deadlines have arrived, and home is far away. This is ordinary, it passes, and the students who talk to someone get through it faster than the ones who go quiet. Our door stays open after you arrive, not just until you enrol.

Still deciding?

Ask the awkward questions.

What if I fail a module. What if I cannot find a flat. What if my family's circumstances change. We would rather answer those now than have you discover them in November.
